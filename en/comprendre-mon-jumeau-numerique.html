The mental model

A brain, an execution, and nothing else

Everything starts from this separation. If you remember only one thing from this document, let it be this one, because everything else follows from it.

Where you start, and why here

This is the first training in the series, and you can read it with your hands in your pockets. Nothing to install, nothing to type, no account to create. You can read it before you even have a twin.

That is on purpose. The next training has you install yours in three quarters of an hour, and you install better what you understand. Almost everyone who got stuck was not held up by a hard step. They just did not know what they were doing.

Later you will connect your twin to what you need, almost always starting with your email. But first, understanding.

Your twin is not a piece of software someone wrote. It is an off-the-shelf AI model, as is, plus a folder of text files. What makes it useful is not the model. It is the folder.

The brain is a set of ordinary text files, the same kind as a note you would write yourself. You can open them, read them, correct them by hand. They say who you are, how you write, what you are working on, and what it has learned from its mistakes.

The execution is a session. It starts, it reads the brain, it works, it writes what it learned into the brain, it dies. It is disposable. Nothing that matters lives inside it.

The practical consequence

Whatever is not written in a file is lost when the window closes. That is the law of how it is built, and it explains almost every behavior you will see: why it saves, why it logs things, why it asks you to correct a file rather than its answer.

The key to everything

Context, the only technical word you need to understand

If this is the only word you understand, you will understand everything else, including behaviors that seem strange. Take three minutes here. They pay for the whole document.

Your twin starts from zero in every conversation. It remembers nothing from yesterday, nothing from the last session, nothing you told it the day before. This is not a flaw we are going to fix. It is how all AI models work, without exception.

So how can it know you? Because at the start of each conversation, a stack of documents is slipped in front of it. It reads them in full, in a few seconds, before you have written your first word.

That stack is what we call context.

Three things follow from this one word, and they explain everything

Why it cannot "know everything" at once. A well-fed brain soon has hundreds, then thousands of files. The stack holds only a small part of them. The rest, it has to go and find, which is why search exists.

Why we do not put everything in its base instructions. Every sentence added there is reread in every conversation, by everyone, forever. It takes up room and it costs. So only what is always useful goes in.

Why what is not written is lost. Your conversation is part of the stack, and the stack is thrown away when the window closes. What was written in a file will be placed on the stack again tomorrow. What stayed in the conversation will not.

The word you will hear, and what it is worth

This stack is measured in tokens, a trade word that roughly means "pieces of words". A token is worth roughly three quarters of a word. Just remember the order of magnitude: the base instructions and the profile take up about fifteen pages, reread at every startup. That is not much, which is why we fight over what goes in.

The comparison

Claude, Cowork, your twin: the question everyone asks

"I already use Claude on the web, and Cowork can work on my computer. What more does the twin bring?" This is the most common question in the program, it is a fair one, and the honest answer is not "the twin is better at everything".

First, everything the Claude app already does

This needs saying honestly and completely, because many people do not know it and because a one-sided comparison backfires on whoever writes it.

It has a memory, and a good one. It retains topics across your conversations, without you having to say "remember". You can view it, correct it, delete parts of it in the settings. It is organized by project, and Cowork shares the same memory as chat mode.

It has permanent instructions. On the Team and Enterprise plans, an organization can set instructions followed in every conversation, which take priority over personal preferences. A project can also carry its own, and be shared with the whole organization.

It works without you. Cowork can run scheduled tasks in the cloud, at their own pace, even with the computer off and the app closed. Daily briefing, weekly report, using your connectors.

And it connects cleanly. Connectors use standard authentication. Claude never sees your password and inherits your own permissions in the service.

In other words, most of the differences people assume do not exist. There are four left, and they are real.

Difference 1. The brain is a folder of files, and it is yours

This is the fundamental difference, and everything else follows from it.

The app's memory lives inside the product. It is excellent, but it stays there. A twin's brain is a folder of text files that you can copy, take with you, open in any editor, and hand to someone else the day you change roles.

Three concrete consequences.

  • A dated, attributed history. Every change is recorded. You can ask "when did we decide this, and why" and get the answer.
  • Nothing gets erased by accident. A bad correction can be found and undone.
  • Changing models costs nothing. The brain is text. If another model becomes better tomorrow, it reads it as is.

Difference 2. What there is to search, and how it is found

An AI can only find what it has been given. So the material matters more than the engine.

The app searches what you have uploaded and what it has retained from your exchanges. That is useful, and it is limited by what you thought to give it.

Your twin searches everything the program has accumulated: decisions logged with their date, written methods, and above all corrections with the reason for each one. This body of knowledge grows with every session and nothing in it is ever destroyed. The archives are part of what gets searched, because archiving is not forgetting.

And it really knows how to search it. Three methods run in parallel: exact words, meaning, and links between notes. The answer is never "read this file". It is "read this passage, right here". We come back to this in detail later.

The misunderstanding to avoid, and it is widespread

People often think the advantage would be having more context. That is wrong, it is actually the opposite. Bloated context degrades results: the right information drowns in it, and you pay for that volume in every exchange.

That is why the list of its memory holds only one line per memory, never the content. It fetches the whole note only when it is useful.

The advantage is not quantity, it is accuracy. What is always there was chosen by us, and what gets added was fetched from exactly the right place.

The part that cannot be copied

Everyone will end up with a search engine, and they will all improve. What cannot be copied is the body of knowledge. Three months of logged decisions, methods and corrections exist nowhere else and cannot be caught up on by buying a product.

The advantage is not the search mechanism. It is what there is to search, and that grows while you work.

Difference 3. It carries CODE, not just text

This is the difference you feel most from day to day.

A memory retains. Instructions describe. A twin runs tools written for REAL systems, and above all for their flaws.

The example worth a thousand speeches. The standard email connector cannot read an attachment. It gives the name, and stops there. That creates the illusion of having read everything, when the real content is often entirely in the attached documents. So the twin has an in-house tool that fetches the text inside.

Your starting brain comes with about a dozen, written one by one as someone ran into each problem, and the number grows with every update. Sending an email without the links being mangled. Reading five accounting systems. Creating an invoice. Signing a document. Pulling the hours out of a timesheet before someone's departure erases them.

The sentence to remember from this section

The value is not in the AI model. It is in patiently filling the gaps between the model and your real systems. Everyone has the model. Not these tools, and each one cost someone a frustration.

And because they live in a shared template, a gap filled once is filled for everyone who has a twin, without anyone having to send you a file.

Difference 4. Autonomous work writes into the brain

Both can work without you. The difference is in what is left afterward.

A scheduled task produces a deliverable, a report, a summary, and that is very good. When the twin works alone, it produces a deliverable and it writes into its brain: the project moved forward, the decision logged, the follow-up set. The next day's session inherits all of it.

That is the difference between work that repeats and work that accumulates.

The vault, and what you need to understand

Standard connectors connect cleanly, without you giving a password. But our in-house tools do need keys: an app password to read a mailbox, a key for a service that has no connector.

Those values live in a vault, away from anything that gets said in conversation. The twin knows which one to use without ever displaying it, and it will never ask you to paste one into the conversation. A key pasted into a chat is a burned key: it stays in the history and ends up in a screenshot. If it ever does ask, that is a flaw. Refuse and report it.

When the app is the RIGHT choice, and it has to be said

The twin is not better at everything, and pretending otherwise would waste your time.

A one-off question, a rewording, a text to rough out, a search unrelated to your projects: open Claude and ask. It is faster, there is nothing to install, and you do not need everything described here.

The twin takes the lead on what you do often, with your own tools and your own projects. That is where written methods, in-house tools and autonomous work pay off. For the rest, the app does the job very well.

And the two together, in practice

Most people end up using both. The app for thinking fast and for one-off things, the twin for what requires knowing your projects, your tools and your way of working. This is not a choice to make once and for all.

Startup, step by step

What happens when you run the command

Now that we know what context is, here is what fills that stack. You type one line, and six things happen before it says hello.

Here is the line. It is the same every day, and it is the only one.

The everyday command
claude --dangerously-skip-permissions --remote-control

The first word starts your twin. The second tells it not to ask your permission before every action, and the last one, --remote-control, makes the session reachable from the web, your phone and the other sessions on your computer. That is what lets the agents talk to each other. Without this option, it asks you dozens of questions per session, you end up saying yes without reading, and that protects no one. You remain responsible for watching, and Ctrl+C stops it at any moment. What does not change: nothing goes out without your approval. That rule lives in its brain and the option does not touch it.

Where to type it, and the trick that saves retyping it

In the terminal, at the bottom of Visual Studio Code, with your jumeau folder open. And the next day, you do not retype it: the up arrow on your keyboard brings back the previous command. Up arrow, Enter.

The distinction between the two columns is what to remember. What is placed on the stack automatically, it cannot forget, since it is there before it starts thinking. Everything else, it has to remember to do, and a twin that forgets one of its first actions works on an outdated brain without knowing it.

That is why we automate what must be guaranteed instead of asking it to do it. The profile is the clearest example.

The six steps, in plain English

1. Its base instructions. A file called CLAUDE.md, its constitution. Its reflexes, its prohibitions, its rules of form. It is the one document it cannot fail to read.

2. The list of what it retained. Not the memories themselves, only their title, one line each. Like the table of contents of a binder. When a line seems useful, it opens the matching note. That is what lets it have three hundred memories without cluttering the stack.

3. Your profile, the time and the place. Placed by small automatic programs called hooks. The word simply means they fire on their own at a precise moment, without being called. The time is refreshed with every message, because an AI has no clock and would get it wrong saying "this morning".

4. It fetches its up-to-date brain. Your brain is backed up online, and it may have been changed since your last session, by you on another computer or by an automatic overnight job. So it brings down the latest version before starting. Without this step, it would be working on yesterday's snapshot.

5. It checks where its memory lives. The memories it writes must land in the backed-up folder, not in some corner of your hard drive. It makes sure of that and repairs it if needed. The reason is simple: a lost computer must never take a twin's memory with it.

6. It updates itself. It checks whether its tools have been improved since yesterday and fetches what is new. Your own content is never touched, only the mechanics change.

An instruction is not a mechanism

A twin that is asked to read its profile can work a whole day on your projects without having opened it, and no one sees it, because nothing breaks. That is why the profile is placed on the stack automatically. What must be guaranteed is not requested, it is automated.

The framework

The four layers of memory

A human brain does not keep every second in mind. It consolidates: the episode becomes a principle, and the episode can still be recalled when a cue awakens it. The twin is built exactly this way, and this is the program's vocabulary.

Context
The stack of documents placed in front of it in every conversation. It reads it in full before you speak, and it has a maximum size.
A token
The unit that measures this stack. About three quarters of a word. The base instructions plus the profile take up about fifteen pages of it.
Working memory
What it has in mind at this moment, meaning the contents of this stack. It costs in every exchange, so only always-active rules live there.
Semantic memory
Facts and principles detached from their episode. Every correction you make becomes a durable rule, with the reason. This is what replicates you.
Episodic memory
Events that happened, with their dates. Large, never destroyed: archiving is not forgetting. You reach it through a cue, not by rereading it.
Procedural memory
Know-how. A playbook, a skill, a tool. It is not told, it is run.

The four mechanisms that connect them

The layers are not enough. The mechanisms are what do the replicating.

Consolidation the episode becomes a principle

In humans, the hippocampus replays the day during the night. Here, it is the consolidation runs and each correction you make that become a written rule. That is what replicates you: the tenth decision gets made on its own because the first nine were consolidated.

Cued recall a cue awakens the memory

The brain search, the navigation map, git log. You do not need to remember where the information is, only to have a piece of it.

Active forgetting leaving the hot path without losing anything

Old logs leave working memory but stay in episodic memory. The equivalent of sorting during sleep. We forget to decide better, we never destroy.

Metacognition knowing what you know

The catalog of its tools, the map of its brain, its roadmap. Knowing what it knows, what it can do, and what it lacks.

The rule for deciding, and why it matters

When a piece of information arrives, the real question is where it lives. An always-active rule goes up into working memory, and then costs in every session for everyone. A durable principle becomes a note. A dated event goes into the project's file. A piece of know-how becomes a tool.

Getting it wrong costs on both sides. Too high, and you waste context at every startup. Too low, and the information cannot be found when it is time to decide.

YOU decide its scope

Your starting brain is neutral. It does not assume your twin will be for work, for your personal life, or for both. The scope is yours to set, because the folder is yours: it is on your account, private, and no one else has access to it.

Many people end up putting both in, and then keep their projects in two separate spaces. It is not required, it is simply handy when the same day mixes a quote and a doctor's appointment.

The only rule that counts: if you mix the two, it takes your personal life into account to weigh your time and workload, but none of it ever comes out in a professional message. The wall is not in how things are filed, it is in what goes out.

This is a real difference from twins built inside a company. Those are work tools, with no personal side, because their folder belongs to the company and can be taken back. Yours cannot.

A playbook and a skill are not the same thing

Both contain a working method, and that is where the confusion starts. The difference is not what is inside, it is WHO triggers it.

A playbook it consults it

It is a cookbook on the shelf. "Here is how to prepare a meeting with a manager", "here is how to handle an email". It recognizes the situation, goes and reads the right page, and applies it. You have nothing to ask for, and you can write one yourself for your way of working.

When the situation comes up, on its own initiative.

A skill you trigger it

It is a button. You call it by name, and the complete method enters the conversation all at once, with its steps in order. It does not choose to read it, it runs it from start to finish.

At the exact moment you type its name, like /consolidation.

Why both exist, and when to choose one or the other.

A playbook fits when the method must apply at the right moment without you thinking about it, and when it can vary from one person to another. It is available knowledge.

A skill fits when the procedure has steps that must not be skipped, and must run the same way for everyone. That is why skills come down from the template, identical on every twin, whereas a playbook can be yours.

An example that makes the difference obvious

/consolidation is a skill, and it could not have been a playbook. It is the session close, the one that sorts what was learned and writes it in the right place. If it could "forget to consult it", it would be useless, and the end of a session, when everyone is in a hurry, is exactly when steps get skipped. You type its name, the procedure enters in full, and it runs in order.

On the other hand, how to prepare a meeting is a playbook. It has to think of it on its own when you say "prepare my Monday", and your way of doing it may differ from a colleague's.

CLAUDE.md, and why it must not grow

It is the constitution. It is loaded in full, in every session, forever. That makes it the most powerful and most expensive place in the brain.

The trap of the growing file

Everything put in it costs at every startup, for every person. And there is worse: when CLAUDE.md copies what lives elsewhere, it hides the aging of the other file. It has happened: a CLAUDE.md had ended up copying part of the profile, so the profile rotted for months without the slightest symptom, because another file was carrying the load in its place.

The resulting rule applies to the whole brain: a file holds what it is the source of, and points to the source of everything else. Two files that say the same thing always end up contradicting each other.

The profile, the foundation

Four files, injected at startup. They say who you are, what you aim for, how you write, and your life outside work if you want it to cover that.

A twin is worth exactly what its profile is worth. It is the part of the brain that makes the difference between a generic assistant and someone who writes like you and weighs things like you.

The profile holds what is STABLE, and points to the rest

People, structures, standing mandates, your way of deciding: all of that is stable and lives in the profile. A target figure, a health status, an amount, a deadline move: they live in their project's file and the profile only points to them.

That is the discipline that keeps a profile from going stale. A profile that copies a status is wrong the next day; a profile that points stays right.

Cued recall

How it finds information

A living brain ends up with thousands of files. It is impossible to read them all for each question, and reading at random costs a lot for nothing. Here is how it finds things.

The semantic path deserves a word, because it is the one that surprises people. Your twin turns each section of the brain into a list of numbers that represents its meaning. Your question gets the same treatment, and it compares them. That is what lets it find the right note when you ask a question that has no word in common with it.

This calculation happens entirely on your side

The model that turns text into numbers runs on your computer, with no server and no network call. Neither your notes nor your questions leave the machine to be indexed. And only the changed sections are recalculated, so it is fast.

If this model is not installed, search falls back to words only and says so. It never pretends.

This database is never the truth

It is built from your files, never the other way around. If you erase it, it rebuilds it on its own in about ten seconds at the next question. The truth is always your text files. The database is only a shortcut for finding them quickly.

Consolidation

How what it learns moves up

This is the question that matters most, because it decides whether your twin improves or stagnates.

Three things happen, and none of them is automatic by magic.

You correct it in a file, not in the conversation. When it gets the tone or the method wrong, ask it to correct the relevant file. A correction made only in the answer is lost when the window closes. A correction written in a note never has to be made again.

It saves when there is a block of value. Not after every sentence, that would be noise. A finished deliverable, a decision made, a project moved forward. Each save is dated and can be found again: that is your episodic memory being written.

It closes the session. That is the only moment when what was learned in the conversation gets sorted and filed in the right place. Without it, the window closes and everything that was not written disappears.

The mechanism that protects everything else

The twin's memory lives in the folder backed up online, not in some corner of your hard drive. This is checked at every startup, and repaired if needed.

The reason is simple, and it is a program rule: two computers, or one lost computer, must never cost a twin its memory. What is backed up nowhere exists only by accident.

Its hands

The toolbox

A model that only talks is not much use. What makes your twin useful is its arms, and there are two kinds.

Connectors MCP

Email, calendar, files, messaging, tasks, and depending on the case accounting or CRM. They plug in and they work, but they have limits, and those limits are often silent.

What the market provides.

In-house scripts tools/

About a dozen to start, maintained together and added to over time. Each one fills a specific gap in a connector. That is where the value is, not in the model.

Where the real work is.

The most useful lesson in this whole document

The email connector cannot read an attachment. It gives the file name and stops there, which creates the illusion of having read everything, when the real content is often entirely in the attachments. Hence an in-house reader that fetches the text of the attached documents.

The same connector wraps every link in a redirect URL, so any email carrying a link goes out through an in-house sending script.

The value is not in the model. It is in patiently filling the gaps between the model and your real systems. And each gap filled once serves all twins, because the tools move up to the template.

It updates itself

At every startup, your twin checks the template and fetches the tools improved since last time. Your content is never touched, only the mechanics come down. You have nothing to ask for and you cannot fall behind.

In practice

Starting a session that accomplishes something

The mechanics are in place. What is left is how you use them, and a few reflexes change everything.

One session, one subject the framing

A conversation that jumps from one project to another produces average work everywhere. Open one session per subject: it will load the right context and go further.

Tell it where to start the entry point

"Look at project X" or "read the email thread from Y" beats letting it search. You give it the cue, its recall does the rest.

Correct right away the loop

A wrong tone, a method that does not suit you: say so on the spot and ask for it to go in the file. Thirty seconds you will never have to spend again.

Close the end

End with /consolidation. That is the moment when what the session learned gets sorted, written and saved. A session that is not closed lets what it learned die with the window.

Good prompting, in four reflexes

Give the goal, not just the task. "Prepare a brief for my meeting with X, I want to decide whether we continue the project" produces something different from "summarize project X". It can weigh things when it knows what is at stake.

Say what you do not want. Constraints count as much as the request. A length, a tone, a recipient not to mention, a source not to use.

Ask it to verify rather than assert. "Go look at the real data" and "tell me what you are not sure about" completely change the quality of what you get. It is allowed to say it does not know. That is actually a rule in its brain.

Let it answer you with a question. When it asks for a clarification instead of guessing, that is the intended behavior, not a weakness. Making things up is the one flaw you cannot recover from.

What will never change

Nothing goes out without your approval. An email, a message, a write to a tool: it prepares a draft and waits. This rule lives in its brain and no technical option gets around it. If it acts without asking, that is a flaw, and it must be reported.

To finish

This document will change

The mechanics described here are young and they change every week.

What is described here is a state, not a final truth. The profile placed at startup, search by meaning, the automations that connect themselves: each one came after some friction, and others will follow.

Two things follow from this for you.

If what you see does not match this document, say so. It means either the mechanics moved ahead without the document following, or there is a flaw on your side. Both are worth knowing.

And most important: almost all the improvements described here were born from friction met by someone using it. Not from an idea in a meeting. Your irritation is raw material.
